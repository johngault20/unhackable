# Password-pattern learning examples (fictional patterns only)

These are abstract patterns, not usable passwords. Do not copy them as credentials. The examples show why a password can look complicated while remaining predictable.

| Pattern (not a password) | Why it is weak or stronger | Safer lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `common-word + year` | Familiar word plus a date is predictable and often reused. | Use a password manager to create a unique random value. |
| `name + birthday` | Personal details may be discoverable or shared across services. | Avoid names, dates, pets, teams, and other personal facts. |
| `keyboard-walk` | Adjacent keyboard characters form an easy-to-guess pattern. | Prefer random generation or unrelated passphrase words. |
| `common-word with @/0/1 substitutions` | Familiar substitutions are well-known and do not reliably make a common word unpredictable. | Do not rely on cosmetic complexity; prioritize uniqueness and length. |
| `same-secret-on-many-sites` | A leak at one service can put other accounts at risk. | Use a different credential for every service. |
| `unrelated-word-word-word-word` | A long passphrase can be easier to remember when words are unrelated and not a quote or personal phrase. | Use sufficient length and uniqueness; let a manager generate it if possible. |
| `manager-generated-random-value` | Randomly generated unique values are difficult to memorize but a manager can store them. | Protect the manager account with MFA and a recovery plan. |

## Important caveat

No pattern table or strength meter can establish that a password is safe. Attack feasibility depends on whether it is exposed or reused, the service's defenses and password storage, MFA, rate limits, and the attacker's access. Do not try to measure a real password by submitting login attempts. Use a reputable password manager and MFA instead.
