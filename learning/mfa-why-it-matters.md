# Why MFA matters beyond password length

A password is one proof of identity. Multifactor authentication (MFA) adds another independent proof, such as a device-bound credential, security key, authenticator approval, or one-time code. If a password is guessed, reused, phished, or leaked, a second factor can make account takeover harder.

## MFA options, broadly

- **Passkeys and security keys:** Often provide strong phishing resistance because authentication is bound to the legitimate site or device. Keep account recovery options secure.
- **Authenticator app codes or prompts:** Usually stronger than password-only access. Approve only sign-ins you initiated; unexpected prompts can indicate someone knows the password or is trying to trick you.
- **SMS codes:** Can provide an extra barrier, but phone-number takeover and message interception are concerns. Prefer stronger options when available; using SMS can still be preferable to no MFA.
- **Email codes:** Better than password-only in some situations, but protection depends on the security of the email account itself.

## What MFA does not do

MFA does not make an account invulnerable. It may not stop malware on an already compromised device, session theft, recovery-channel abuse, social engineering, or every provider-side failure. Keep devices and browsers updated, secure recovery accounts, and review provider sign-in activity.

## Practical setup

1. Start with your primary email, password manager, financial accounts, and cloud storage.
2. Enable the strongest method that is practical and supported—prefer passkeys or security keys when appropriate.
3. Keep recovery codes somewhere safe and separate from the account device.
4. Never approve a prompt you did not initiate or tell a caller a one-time code.
5. For “Was this you?” notifications, open the provider's official app or type its known address yourself. Do not use unexpected message links.

A long unique password and MFA work together: the password reduces guessing and reuse risk, while MFA adds a separate hurdle. Neither replaces updates, vigilance, account recovery hygiene, or tested backups.
